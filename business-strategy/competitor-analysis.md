# Competitor Analysis — Prince William County Home Care

**Prepared:** 23 November 2026
**Method:** Review of publicly available agency websites, directory listings, and published pricing. No mystery shopping, no private data, no solicitation of client reviews was performed for this concept. Competitor facts should be re-verified before use in sales materials.

> **Evidence standard.** "Listed" means the agency appears in a public directory or on its own website [S16][S17]. "Published rate" means the agency itself published the figure. "Assumption" is a planning judgment. This analysis deliberately does **not** characterize competitors' quality, because public review samples are small and not comparable.

---

## 1. Market structure

The county's home care supply has four layers:

1. **National franchises** — Homewatch CareGivers, Visiting Angels, FirstLight Home Care, Home Helpers, Comfort Keepers, BrightStar Care and similar brands. They bring brand trust, formal training systems, and higher overhead.
2. **Independent licensed agencies** — local, often nurse-owned or family-owned, with 10–80 caregivers. Pricing and service quality vary widely.
3. **Registry and marketplace models** — the Prince William Area Agency on Aging's Care Registry, Care.com, and similar platforms. They connect families to independent caregivers but do not employ, supervise, or insure them [S10].
4. **Informal and community care** — family, church networks, neighbors, and senior center programs. The true default competitor.

**Verified.** The Area Agency on Aging's Care Registry explicitly states that it does not certify, train, license, or interview listed companions, and that companions remain independent contractors [S10]. This is an important consumer-education contrast: an agency employs, trains, insures, supervises, and can be held accountable.

## 3. Price positioning

| Benchmark | Rate / cost | Source |
|---|---|---|
| National median professional caregiver rate, 2025 | ~$35/hr | CareScout/Genworth [S14] |
| Virginia median home care rate, 2026 guide | ~$35/hr | A Place for Mom [S15] |
| Manassas franchise published range | $35–$42/hr | Visiting Angels [S16] |
| Woodbridge home care average, monthly | ~$5,339/month | Caring.com [S18] |
| National median annual home health aide cost, 2024 | $77,792 | Genworth/CareScout [S13] |
| National median annual homemaker cost, 2024 | $75,504 | Genworth/CareScout [S13] |

**Interpretation.** A $5,339 monthly figure is roughly 30 hours/week at ~$41/hour, or 36 hours/week at ~$34/hour. The local price band is therefore broad enough that a new entrant can compete on value without racing to the bottom.

**Recommendation.** Launch at **$32 companion / $36 personal care / $34 respite**, with a 4-hour minimum and no weekly minimum. That sits at or slightly below the local midpoint, supports above-market caregiver wages, and leaves room for a 3–5% annual adjustment. Validate with a bottom-up cost model before publishing.

## 4. Where competitors are strong

- **Brand trust.** National franchises reduce perceived risk for first-time buyers.
- **Service breadth.** Most offer companion through 24-hour care, and some offer skilled services through affiliated agencies.
- **Established referral relationships.** Hospitals and senior communities already know the major brands.
- **Training infrastructure.** Franchise systems provide standardized onboarding and compliance materials.
- **Geographic coverage.** Larger agencies can staff farther-out communities.

A new agency should not try to beat these on breadth or brand recognition in year one.

## 5. Observable gaps and unmet needs

These are **opportunity hypotheses** supported by the structure of the market, not verified complaints about named competitors.

| Gap | Why it persists | How to exploit it |
|---|---|---|
| **Rate opacity** | Many agencies quote only after an in-home assessment | Publish rates and the minimum visit on the website |
| **Coordinator turnover** | Large agencies route calls through central intake | Name one coordinator per family; publish office hours |
| **Inconsistent caregivers** | Scheduling software optimizes coverage, not fit | Match on language, temperament, and routine; introduce a named backup |
| **Weak family communication** | Caregivers document for compliance, not for families | Send a short written Friday brief; make it skimmable |
| **Hospital discharge gap** | Medicare covers skilled visits, not the practical first 72 hours [S22] | Sell a flat 3-day non-clinical bridge package |
| **Language access** | Bilingual staff are scarce and expensive | Hire bilingual coordinators; publish Spanish materials only when staffed |
| **Contract friction** | Some agencies require long commitments or large deposits | No long-term contract; 7-day notice |
| **Scope confusion** | Families do not know home care from home health | Publish a clear "what we do / what we do not do" page |
| **Registry risk** | Registry caregivers are unscreened independent contractors [S10] | Educate families on employment, insurance, and supervision differences |
| **Rural travel** | Haymarket/Gainesville visits carry windshield time | Quote travel transparently and cluster schedules |

## 7. Indirect competitors

| Alternative | Appeal | Limitation | Our response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family caregiving | Free, trusted | Unsustainable; caregiver burnout | Respite and coaching, not replacement |
| Care Registry / Care.com | Low hourly cost | No training, insurance, supervision, or backup [S10] | Explain total risk and employer obligations |

| Assisted living | Socialization, 24-hour staff | Cost, loss of home, limited autonomy | Aging-in-place support and home safety modifications |
| Adult day health care | Structured daytime supervision | Transportation and hours | Combine with companion care and transport |
| Medicare home health | Covered skilled care | Short-term, homebound, physician-ordered; no custodial care [S22] | Partner, do not duplicate |

## 8. Competitive conclusions

1. **Do not compete on breadth.** The county already has larger agencies with wider menus.
2. **Compete on the first 30 days.** Most churn happens early. A deliberate match, a 72-hour check-in, and a 14-day review are cheap and rare.
3. **Make the invisible visible.** Publish rates, backup policy, response times, and scope. Most competitors do not.
4. **Own the hospital-to-home niche.** It is urgent, referable, and poorly served by non-clinical agencies.
5. **Treat bilingual coordination as a hiring plan, not a slogan.** Only advertise Spanish-language service when a qualified coordinator is actually on staff.
6. **Stay local.** Ten communities within one county is a feature: shorter drives, better reliability, stronger referral density.
