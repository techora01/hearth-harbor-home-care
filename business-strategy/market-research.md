# Market Research — Home Care in Prince William County, Virginia

**Prepared:** 23 November 2026
**Prepared for:** Heart & Harbor Homecare (concept)
**Geography:** Prince William County, Manassas, Manassas Park, Woodbridge, Gainesville, Haymarket, Dumfries, Occoquan, Lake Ridge, Dale City, Bristow
**Status:** Secondary research synthesis. Figures are current as of the cited publication date; re-verify before fundraising or launch.

> **How to read this document.** Statements marked **Verified** come directly from a cited public source. Statements marked **Assumption** are planning estimates created for this project and must not be presented as facts. Statements marked **Recommendation** are business judgments.

## 1. Executive summary

Prince William County is the second-most populous county in Virginia, with roughly 503,000 residents in 2025 [S2]. About 60,900 residents were 65 or older in 2024 — 12.3% of the population, well below Virginia's 17.8% [S2]. That lower share is not a weakness in the market; it is a timing signal. The county's large 45–64 cohort is approaching retirement, and the absolute number of older adults is growing even as the county's overall population grows faster.

**Verified.** The county is exceptionally diverse: 26.8% of residents identify as Hispanic or Latino and 20.1% as Black or African American, compared with 20.0% and 12.6% nationally [S2]. Language access and culturally congruent caregiving are therefore mainstream requirements in this market, not niche add-ons.

**Verified.** Virginia families provide about $27 billion of unpaid care each year [S12]. Nationally, the median non-medical caregiver rate reached about $35 per hour in 2025, and the annual median cost of home health aide care was $77,792 in 2024 [S13][S14]. Local published rates around Manassas run $35–$42 per hour [S16].

**Recommendation.** The opportunity is not "more home care." It is **reliable, communicative, transparently priced home care** for adult children who live nearby but work full time, and for the county's large multilingual population. The market is crowded with franchises and small owner-operated agencies; the gap is operational trust, not service breadth.

The county's older-adult share is lower than the state average because of sustained in-migration of younger families. The absolute base is still large: 60,900 people aged 65+ is comparable to a mid-sized city. Growth in the 75+ and 85+ cohorts — the groups most likely to need hands-on help — is the more relevant demand signal than the overall 65+ percentage.

### 2.2 Household and caregiver context

**Verified.** AARP Virginia estimates the value of unpaid family caregiving in the Commonwealth at $27 billion per year [S12]. A 2026 AARP report found working caregivers face substantial lifetime earnings losses, and another 2026 report put average out-of-pocket caregiving costs above $11,500 per year [S12].

**Assumption.** A large share of Prince William's older adults live in owner-occupied suburban homes and want to remain there. The county's housing stock — 1960s ramblers in Dale City, newer construction in Gainesville and Bristow, historic buildings in Occoquan — creates a wide range of accessibility conditions. No single "aging in place" checklist fits all of them.

**Recommendation.** Treat the adult child as the primary buyer and the older adult as the primary user. Marketing should speak to the daughter or son managing a full-time job, not only to the senior. The purchase is often triggered by a fall, a hospital discharge, or a sibling disagreement.

## 3. Demand drivers

### 3.1 Verified drivers

1. **Aging in place preference.** The Prince William Area Agency on Aging explicitly frames in-home assistance as a way to delay or avoid assisted living and nursing home placement [S10].
2. **Caregiver strain.** The Area Agency on Aging operates caregiver support groups and caregiver assistance programs, indicating recognized local demand [S11].
3. **Hospital transitions.** Discharge planning creates urgent, short-notice demand for non-clinical help at home. Medicare covers only short-term skilled home health, not custodial or companion care [S22].
4. **Diversity and language.** More than a quarter of county residents are Hispanic or Latino [S2]; bilingual coordination is a practical market requirement.
5. **A fragmented local supply.** Public directories list dozens of agencies in Manassas and Prince William County, ranging from national franchises to solo operators [S16][S17].

### 3.2 Market sizing — planning envelope, not a verified TAM

There is no public, county-level spend figure for private-pay home care. The following is a **transparent planning model**, with every assumption labeled.

| Input | Low case | Base case | High case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residents 65+ | 60,900 | 60,900 | 60,900 |
| Share using paid home care in a year (assumption) | 2% | 3.5% | 5% |
| Annual clients | 1,218 | 2,132 | 3,045 |
| Average paid hours/week (assumption) | 8 | 12 | 16 |
| Blended hourly rate (assumption) | $34 | $36 | $38 |
| Implied annual market | ~$17.2M | ~$47.9M | ~$96.3M |

**Interpretation.** A $17M–$96M county-level private-pay envelope is plausible but unverified. A new agency capturing 0.5%–1.5% of the base case would generate roughly **$240k–$720k** in annual revenue. At a $36 rate and 12 hours/week, one full client produces about **$22,500 per year**; 30 clients produce about **$674,000**. These are planning figures, not forecasts.

**Recommendation.** Build the operating model around 25–40 active clients within 12–18 months, not hundreds. A small agency with strong retention can be viable at that scale; a large geographic footprint is not required.

## 6. Demand for non-medical vs. skilled services

**Verified.** Virginia defines a home care organization (HCO) as an entity providing home health services, personal care services, or pharmaceutical services in a person's residence [S4]. Personal care includes assistance with activities of daily living [S4]. Skilled home health — nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology — is a different, clinically licensed service line; Medicare requires a physician order and a Medicare-certified agency [S22].

**Verified.** Medicare does **not** pay for 24-hour care, meal delivery, homemaker services unrelated to a care plan, or custodial/personal care when that is the only care needed [S22]. This is the single most important consumer-education point for a non-medical agency.

**Recommendation.** Position the business firmly in non-medical home care. Do not advertise skilled nursing, therapy, wound care, or medication administration. Build referral relationships with Medicare-certified home health agencies, then serve the custodial needs Medicare does not cover. This is complementary, not competitive.

## 7. Local referral ecosystem

| Referral source | Why they refer | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital discharge planners (Sentara Northern Virginia, UVA Health Prince William, Novant Haymarket) | Patients need non-clinical help Medicare will not cover | Provide a one-page scope sheet, 72-hour bridge protocol, and a single intake number |
| Skilled home health agencies | They provide clinical visits; families need custodial help between visits | Formal reciprocal referral agreement; never duplicate clinical care |
| Primary care practices and geriatricians | Families ask "where do I start?" | Lunch-and-learn packets; no kickbacks |
| Prince William Area Agency on Aging | Care coordination and caregiver support | Register as a private-pay resource; attend caregiver events |
| Senior centers | Trusted community touchpoints | Educational talks on fall prevention and care planning |
| Elder law attorneys and financial planners | Clients need care plans and documentation | Referral cards; co-host family planning workshops |
| Assisted living and memory care communities | Residents sometimes transition home or need supplemental help | Clarify facility contracting and supervision rules first |
| Rehab and skilled nursing facilities | Discharge to home | Same 72-hour bridge offer |
| Faith communities and cultural associations | Trusted messengers, especially for multilingual families | Spanish-language workshops; parish nurse relationships |
| Long-term care insurance agents | Policyholders need covered providers | Documentation packet and direct billing capability |

**Recommendation.** Do not pay for referrals. Virginia and federal fraud-and-abuse rules, plus Medicare/Medicaid inducement rules, make per-referral payments hazardous. Build reciprocity through responsiveness and education instead.

## 8. Competitive opportunity

**Verified.** Public directories show a crowded field: national franchises (Homewatch CareGivers, Visiting Angels, FirstLight, Home Helpers) and independent agencies in Manassas and Woodbridge [S16][S17]. Published local rates cluster around $35–$42 per hour [S16].

**Assumption.** Most local agencies compete on the same generic claims: compassionate caregivers, affordable care, licensed and insured. Few publish rates, backup-caregiver policies, or a written family-update cadence.

**Recommendation.** Compete on **operational transparency**:
- Publish hourly rates and minimums.
- Name the coordinator and the backup caregiver.
- Send a written weekly family brief.
- Publish a clear scope statement (what is not provided).
- Offer a real 72-hour hospital-to-home bridge.

These are difficult for a large franchise to change quickly and cheap for a small agency to deliver consistently.

## 9. Risks and uncertainties

| Risk | Evidence / reason | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Caregiver labor shortage | National home care staffing pressure; local wages compete with retail and warehouse work | Above-market wages, consistent schedules, paid training, small caseloads |
| Regulatory misclassification | Virginia licenses personal care; "non-medical" is not a blanket exemption [S5][S8] | Obtain HCO license before delivering personal care; counsel review |
| Price sensitivity | $35–$42/hr is a real household expense | 4-hour minimum, respite packages, LTC insurance documentation |
| Referral concentration | A few hospitals drive much of the urgent demand | Diversify across senior centers, attorneys, faith groups, digital |
| Reputation risk | One bad hire can dominate local reviews | Background checks, supervised onboarding, fast complaint resolution |
| Overexpansion | Drive times erode margins and reliability | Stay within Prince William County for the first 18 months |

## 11. Bottom line

The county has a large, diverse, aging population and a crowded but undifferentiated home care market. The winning position is not the cheapest or the broadest. It is the agency that **answers the phone, sends the same caregiver, explains the bill, and tells the family the truth every week**. That is the thesis the nine website demonstrations are built to express.
